Trauma & Difficult Memories

After a frightening, overwhelming, or deeply distressing experience, people can have reactions involving memories, sleep, concentration, avoidance, mood, or feeling on edge.

Talk to the practiceAll profiles

What you can try

  • Prioritize immediate safety and supportive people when you can.
  • Use grounding: name what you see, hear and feel around you.
  • Go at a pace that feels manageable; trauma work should not be rushed.
  • Consider trauma-informed therapy if memories or reactions keep interfering with life.

How therapy may help

  • The original site lists EMDR as one approach.
  • Trauma-informed work emphasizes safety, collaboration and pacing.
  • A clinician can discuss whether trauma-focused treatment is appropriate.
Do not force yourself to revisit distressing memories alone. A trained clinician can help determine whether and how trauma-focused work fits.

Understand

You are allowed to be unsure.

Online information can give you language for an experience, but it cannot replace an individual assessment. Context, history, safety and goals matter.

Practical guidance

Small, realistic steps beat perfect plans.

Choose one idea that feels safe and manageable. If it makes things worse, stop and seek professional guidance.

Observe

Notice when the pattern appears and what tends to happen next.

Support

Tell one trustworthy person what you have been experiencing.

Discuss

Bring your observations to a qualified professional.

Therapy

Therapy should be collaborative.

Ask the clinician what they recommend, why, what the goals are, and how progress will be reviewed.
